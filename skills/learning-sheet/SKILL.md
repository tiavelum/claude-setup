# Make a learning sheet

## Settle the purpose

1. Ask what the sheet is for when the request leaves it open: to understand and memorise a topic, or to look things up. This skill makes the first; for the second, make a reference document instead.
2. Ask whether the person already pictures the topic in some way, and start from that picture.
3. Write the sheet for the topic in general, and name products or services only as examples, unless the person asks for one particular service.

## Find the scene

1. Choose one scene from everyday life in which every item of the topic has its place, or for a process its step along one route, such as a house with a key cabinet and buildings in town for logins and their recovery.
2. Give every item one name, the term the learner will meet elsewhere, one sentence that places it in the scene and gives it a job no other item has, one icon and one number, and use the same four wherever the item appears. Number the items in the order page one shows them.
3. Sort the items into two to four zones by one rule used for all zones: the places of the scene, such as at home, in town and if lost, the phases of a process, or the groups of roles. Make each item belong to exactly one zone. Where the topic is a sequence, order the items as they happen and make each zone a run of that order. Give each zone one colour and one marker shape that both pages repeat, and a key to the zones on page one.
4. Within a zone, keep items that are alternatives to each other together, and show an item that adds to another beside the item it adds to, never among the alternatives.
5. Before you lay out the pages, have an agent that did not see the scene being made check it as text: give it the topic, the scene, the zones, and each item with its number, name, sentence, zone and a one-line description of its icon. Have it read them as a learner who does not know the topic, check each sentence against public sources, and report each item whose place in the scene is unclear, each part that contradicts itself or the topic, such as an item that needs itself to work or an icon that gives an item a property the real one lacks, and each question the learner would still ask.
6. Apply each finding, or put it to the person with your reason for leaving it, before you go on.

## Lay out the pages

1. Make the sheet as a Design artifact with one artboard per page at A4, 794 × 1123 px, or at Letter, 816 × 1056 px, for a North American reader, so that each artboard prints as one page. Where no Design type is offered, make a document with the same two parts.
2. Set all text at 12 px (9 pt) or larger, dark on a light ground, so that it reads when printed in grayscale.
3. Budget each page's height before writing it, with at least 40 px (about 11 mm) left at every edge, since [many printers leave the outermost millimetres blank](https://support.microsoft.com/en-US/publisher/set-page-margins-for-printing-using-publisher), and a further 40 px spare at the bottom, since the export may break lines differently from your render.
4. Give every column of a table and every card a fixed width, so that no other browser can redistribute the space.
5. Page one tells the scene: the scene as one illustration with a numbered marker for each item it draws and a legend above it that names them, a card with icon, number, name and sentence for each item the scene does not draw, and, where the topic has one contrast that matters most, an illustrated pair for it with both items' numbers. Write page one's sentences in the scene's words only, naming each item by its term and leaving every technical fact to page two.
6. Page two gives the facts, in plain technical language: a table with one row per item of page one, in page one's order and under the same number, name and icon, giving what it is and the two or three properties on which the items differ, such as what it does, what it needs and what happens without it. Group the rows by the sections of page one, under technical titles that carry the zone's marker. Below the table, give the few questions and rules to remember.

## Check the pages

1. Render each page to an image at its print size, with the sheet's own fonts, since another font moves the line breaks. Where the font service is blocked, take the fonts from a package registry.
2. Have an agent that did not see the sheet being made check the pages for print: give it the page images, the page source, the page size, the 40 px edge minimum and the 12 px text minimum. Have it measure each page's content height against the page height and report, with page and place, text that is cut off, overlaps or is smaller than 12 px, content within 40 px of an edge, a legend number without its marker, a zone whose colour or marker shape changes from one place to another, an item whose icon differs between the pages, an item without its number, and a row of page two whose number, name or group differs from page one.
3. Have the same agent check every sentence written after the scene check, such as page two's details, questions and rules, against public sources, and report each one that is wrong, unclear to a learner, or contradicted by another, such as a rule that a row of the table contradicts.
4. Apply each finding, or put it to the person with your reason for leaving it; then render again, look at what changed, and have the same agent check each sentence you rewrote.

## Hand it over

1. Look at both pages as the person's own browser shows them, or as they export, where you can reach either, and fix what is cut off or overlaps.
2. Hand over both pages in a form that prints one page per sheet, and name the paper size they are made for.
3. Where you could not see the pages as the person will, ask them to export both pages and check that nothing is cut off or overlaps.
4. Where no agent could check the scene or the pages, tell the person that no second reader checked them.

## Improve this skill

At the end of each session that makes a learning sheet:

1. List what the person corrected or asked to change while the sheet was made.
2. For each correction that a step of this skill would have avoided, propose that step with its exact text, and ask whether to add it.
